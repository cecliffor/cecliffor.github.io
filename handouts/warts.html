Plantar Wart Treatment

Warts are caused by HPV and can be stubborn, painful, and contagious. Several treatment options exist — and some patients, especially those with diabetes or poor circulation, should see a podiatrist rather than self-treat.

Same-day visits  ·  No referral needed  ·  Laser and in-office options

What Are Plantar Warts?

Plantar warts are rough, sometimes painful growths on the sole of the foot caused by human papillomavirus (HPV). About 1 in 10 people develop warts at some point, and roughly a quarter of those are on the feet. They can spread through shared showers, pool decks, and floors — wearing socks or sandals in those spaces reduces risk.

Many Go Away on Their Own — But Not All

About 65% of warts clear within 2 years and 95% within 4 years without any treatment. That's worth knowing before you commit to anything aggressive.

However, warts that haven't cleared after many months to a year are less likely to go away on their own. And mosaic warts — clusters of many small warts — are especially persistent. For those, treatment makes more sense.

Plantar Warts Are Harder to Treat

A large Cochrane review found no treatment that works particularly well on the sole of the foot. In one well-known randomized trial, only 14% of plantar warts cleared at 12 weeks with either cryotherapy or salicylic acid. That's a fair, evidence-based reason to offer something beyond drugstore remedies for stubborn warts.

Treatment Options at a Glance

  • Salicylic acid (OTC) — daily at home; modest benefit, weaker on feet than hands; labels say not to use with diabetes or poor circulation
  • Cryotherapy (freezing) — in office, every 2–3 weeks; same cure rate as salicylic acid for plantar warts in trials
  • Prescription topicals (5-FU, imiquimod, cidofovir, vitamin D analogs) — at home, usually for stubborn warts
  • Cantharidin-based treatment — applied in office; high cure rates in plantar wart studies
  • Laser therapy — in office, typically every 2–4 weeks; a reasonable option for stubborn or recurring warts
  • Combination plans — pairing home filing/salicylic acid with in-office treatments often works better than either alone

Laser Therapy: An Honest Look

Laser therapy is a reasonable option for stubborn or recurring plantar warts — not a clearly superior one. Here's what the evidence actually shows:

  • How it works: The laser is used to precisely cut away the wart tissue. It removes what's visible while sparing the surrounding skin, and can be used repeatedly if needed.
  • vs. cryotherapy: In the one randomized trial comparing a long-pulsed Nd:YAG laser to cryotherapy (150 patients), cure rates at 6 months were nearly identical — 59.5% with laser vs. 57.3% with cryotherapy — though the laser cleared warts somewhat faster.
  • Clearance rates in studies: Overall 47–100%, averaging about 79% across plantar wart studies — but most of those are uncontrolled.
  • Fewer sessions with home care: Using salicylic acid before laser didn't raise cure rates, but it cut the average number of sessions — supporting a combined plan with filing before each session.
  • Who benefits most: Combining laser with other treatments may help immunosuppressed patients and warts that resist treatment or keep coming back.
  • Side effects: Some discomfort during and after treatment is expected. Pain, blistering, crusting, and temporary skin color changes are the most common. Scarring is uncommon but possible.

What We Don't Claim

Laser therapy is not "permanent," "100% curative," or "painless." Recurrence rates are similar to standard treatments, and most published clearance figures come from small or uncontrolled studies. We'll talk honestly about what to expect for your specific wart.

When to See a Podiatrist Instead of Self-Treating

  • You have diabetes, poor circulation, or neuropathy — OTC salicylic acid products carry label warnings against use in these patients
  • You have a weakened immune system
  • The wart is painful, spreading, or has been present for many months
  • You have mosaic warts (clusters of many small warts)
  • A growth on your foot might not actually be a wart — a biopsy may be needed

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a plantar wart go away on its own?

Often, yes. About 65% of warts clear within 2 years and 95% within 4 years without treatment. But warts that haven't cleared after many months to a year are less likely to go away on their own, and mosaic warts are especially persistent. In those cases, treatment makes more sense.

Why are plantar warts harder to treat than other warts?

Warts on the sole of the foot respond less well to standard treatments than warts elsewhere. A large Cochrane review found no treatment that works particularly well on plantar warts, and one randomized trial found only 14% cleared at 12 weeks with either cryotherapy or salicylic acid. That's why a treatment plan that goes beyond drugstore remedies is often worthwhile.

Is laser therapy better than cryotherapy for plantar warts?

Not clearly. In the one randomized trial comparing long-pulsed Nd:YAG laser to cryotherapy in 150 patients, cure rates at 6 months were nearly identical (59.5% with laser vs. 57.3% with cryotherapy), though the laser cleared warts somewhat faster. Overall clearance in laser studies ranges from 47% to 100%, but most of those studies are uncontrolled. Laser is a reasonable option for stubborn or recurring warts — not a clearly superior one.

Does laser wart treatment hurt?

Some discomfort is expected during and after treatment. Pain, blistering, crusting, and temporary skin color changes are the most common side effects. Scarring is uncommon. We'll talk through what to expect for your specific wart and how to care for the area afterward.

Can I just use over-the-counter wart remover?

For many healthy people, yes — salicylic acid is a reasonable first step. But the labels on over-the-counter salicylic acid products say they should not be used by people with diabetes or poor circulation. If you have diabetes, neuropathy, poor circulation, or a weakened immune system, you should see a podiatrist rather than self-treat.

When should I see a podiatrist for a wart?

See a podiatrist if you have diabetes, poor circulation, neuropathy, or a weakened immune system; if the wart is painful, spreading, or has been present for many months; if you have mosaic warts; or if a growth on your foot might not actually be a wart. A biopsy may be needed to confirm.
